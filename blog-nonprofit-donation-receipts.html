How Nonprofits Can Send Donation Receipts and Show Donors Where the Money Went

Photo by Michelle Ding on Unsplash

Last Tuesday, a public school teacher in Cebu saw your post about the feeding program. She sent ₱500 through GCash — not a small amount on a teacher's salary — screenshotted the confirmation, and messaged it to your Page with “God bless po.” A volunteer saw it, reacted with a heart, and moved on to the next ninety unread messages. Three months later she still doesn't know whether the money arrived, what it bought, or whether a single child ate because of it. She isn't angry with you. She simply won't give again — and she will never tell you why.

The Silence After the Gift Is What Quietly Costs You

Most small nonprofits in the Philippines receive gifts the same way: a GCash or Maya number and a bank account posted on Facebook, screenshots arriving in the Page inbox at all hours, and one dedicated person writing everything into a notebook or a spreadsheet on her own laptop. It works, until it doesn't. Nobody is sure whether the ₱500 from June was ever recorded, because the volunteer who logged it has since moved to Manila. A regular donor asks what happened to her gift and three staff members give three different answers. And when a corporate partner, a barangay official, or a grant officer asks for a report, someone spends two nights reconstructing eight months of giving from Messenger threads and bank texts. This is the same quiet tax we described in the real cost of sticking with spreadsheets — it isn't laziness, it's a system that was never designed to keep up.

What Transparency Actually Means to a Filipino Donor

It helps to know that most donors are not asking for audited financial statements. They are asking for three much smaller things. First, confirmation that the gift arrived — with their name spelled correctly, because being thanked by the wrong name is worse than not being thanked at all. Second, a plain number: how much came in this quarter, how much went out, and to what. Third, one honest story with a photo, so the amount stops being abstract. Institutional donors need a little more — a formal acknowledgement letter, and often a Certificate of Donation (BIR Form 2322) if your organization is registered as a donee institution — but the individual giving ₱300 a month simply wants to know she wasn't foolish to trust you. Give her those three things and she gives again. Withhold them and she quietly stops, no matter how good the work is.

How to Set Up Receipts and Reporting You Can Actually Sustain

None of this requires accounting software or a finance department. It requires one place where gifts are recorded and one habit of writing back. Here's a practical order to build it in:

  • Give giving a real home on your website. One page that lists your GCash, Maya, and bank details with the exact account name, plus a short form where a donor enters her name, email, amount, and reference number. That form is what turns a screenshot into a record — and it belongs on the kind of site we described in nonprofit website design that earns donor trust.
  • Send a receipt within 48 hours. Keep a short template: date, amount, channel, reference number, what it's designated for, and a genuine thank-you. Sent from your own domain email — not a personal Gmail — so it looks like it came from an organization, not a stranger.
  • Keep one register, not five. A single shared sheet or simple database with columns for date, donor, amount, channel, reference number, purpose, and “receipt sent?” One source of truth that survives a volunteer's resignation.
  • Publish one simple figures page each quarter. Total received, total spent by category, and how many people were served. Three lines and a short paragraph is enough. Donors are far more moved by a modest number you actually published than by a big number they can't verify.
  • Tell one story a month, with a photo and a peso figure. “₱18,400 in July fed 62 children in Sitio Mabuhay for four Saturdays.” Concrete beats inspirational every time.
  • Prepare the formal paperwork before it's demanded. Acknowledgement letters, liquidation reports for LGU or grant-funded projects, and donation certificates for donors claiming deductions — ask your accountant which apply to your registration, then keep blank templates ready.

Start with the receipt. It is the smallest piece and it changes the most: pick a template this week, send it to your next ten donors within two days of their gift, and note in your register that you did. Add the quarterly figures page after that, then the monthly story. Within one giving cycle you'll have something most organizations your size don't — a donor who knows exactly what her money did and expects to hear from you again. It's the same discipline that keeps a volunteer roster from dissolving into group-chat chaos: write it down once, in a place everyone can see.
